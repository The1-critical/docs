---
title: "Code of War"
---

The path of a shinobi is not a straightforward ascent measured in years of service or even in the mastery of dangerous techniques. Rather, it is a complex and often treacherous journey through layers of obligation, reputation, and demonstrated capability, each layer reinforcing the others in an intricate web of expectation and accountability that mirrors the political structures of the great villages themselves. A shinobi who possesses exceptional combat prowess yet lacks the trust of their comrades will find themselves forever confined to the lower ranks, no matter how many enemies they have felled or how many years they have served. Conversely, a warrior whose leadership inspires loyalty but whose combat record remains unremarkable will discover that advancement eludes them, for the bureaucracy demands that those who lead must first prove they can fight. The system is harsh, perhaps brutally so, but it is also fundamentally just, or at least, as just as any system conceived by politicians and soldiers can be, given that both groups are prone to the same human failings of bias, favoritism, and self-interest that have plagued civilization since time immemorial.

The three pillars upon which the entire promotion system rests are not arbitrary restrictions designed to frustrate ambitious shinobi or to maintain an artificially rigid hierarchy. They represent instead the three fundamental ways a shinobi proves their worth to their village and to the Confederacy at large. The first, known as **Certification**, is the formal acknowledgment of skill and knowledge, the paper trail that demonstrates a shinobi has completed the requisite training and passed the necessary examinations. The second is **Field Honour**, the tangible proof of performance under fire, the scars and stories that cannot be fabricated or falsified, the hard-won recognition that comes from surviving the sorts of missions that kill lesser warriors. The third is **Norm**, the intangible yet undeniable weight of reputation and accumulated trust, the respect of those who have stood beside you and decided you are worthy of greater responsibility. Without any one of these three pillars, a shinobi's advancement becomes stalled, blocked not by any official prohibition but by the practical reality that no promotion board will elevate someone who fails to meet all three requirements.

## The Three Pillars of Advancement

### I. Certification—The Measure of Knowledge and Skill

Certifications represent the formal recognition of a shinobi's skills and knowledge, the accumulated evidence of training completed, examinations passed, and competencies demonstrated through rigorous evaluation. These are not merely pieces of paper, though they are often recorded on scrolls stamped with the official seals of the certifying authority. Rather, they embody the accumulated weight of years of study and practice, the formal acknowledgment by the village that a shinobi has met the standards required to perform at a certain level of competence and responsibility. A certification cannot be easily faked, not because the system is perfect, but because the certifying authority is responsible for maintaining the integrity of the records, and any shinobi discovered to have falsified their credentials faces severe consequences, including demotion, exile, or in the most serious cases, execution.

#### Types of Certification:

| Category | Examples | Issuing Authority |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Academy Certification | Graduation from a recognized ninja academy | Village Academy Board |
| Combat Proficiency | Qualification with specific weapon types, mastery of jutsu categories | Village Combat Training Division |
| Specialized Training | Medical certification, sealing technique proficiency, interrogation training | Village Specialized Training Corps |
| Leadership Certification | Squad command training, tactical planning courses | Village Command Training Division |
| Elemental Certification | Demonstrated mastery of an elemental nature | Village Ninjutsu Research Division |
| Prodigy Certification | Recognition of extraordinary clan-specific techniques or abilities | Clan Elders |
| Confederacy Certification | Recognition by the Shinobi Confederacy's central authority | Confederacy Assessment Bureau |

The process of earning a certification varies considerably depending on the type and the issuing authority. Academy certification is typically earned through formal education and comprehensive graduation examinations, which test both theoretical knowledge and practical application across the full range of basic shinobi skills. Combat proficiency certifications are earned through standardized testing, often administered by certified instructors or senior shinobi who evaluate the candidate's performance against established benchmarks. Specialized certifications require dedicated training programs, often lasting several months or even years, followed by rigorous practical examinations that demonstrate the candidate's ability to apply their training in realistic scenarios. Leadership certifications require a combination of classroom instruction and field experience, with candidates expected to demonstrate their ability to command and coordinate others under pressure, even when fatigue and fear threaten to break discipline.

The examination process itself is designed to be both rigorous and revealing. Candidates are evaluated not only on their technical proficiency but also on their composure under pressure, their ability to adapt to unexpected challenges, and their judgment in high-stakes situations. The examiners are trained to identify weaknesses that might not be apparent in a controlled testing environment, and they will deliberately push candidates to their limits to see how they respond. A shinobi who panics during a certification examination is likely to panic during a real mission when lives hang in the balance, and the village cannot afford to send warriors into the field who cannot be trusted to maintain their composure when circumstances turn desperate.

#### The Weight of Paper

The value of a certification depends heavily upon its source, a reality that has created significant disparities in the advancement opportunities available to different shinobi. Certifications from major villages carry considerably more weight than those from smaller settlements, not because the quality of training is necessarily superior in absolute terms, but because the major villages have accumulated decades or even centuries of institutional knowledge, rigorous evaluation processes, and resources that frontier dojos simply cannot replicate. A certification from the Konohagakure Academy is worth significantly more in the eyes of a promotion board than one from a small, independent dojo, even if the students of that dojo are individually more skilled. The Shinobi Confederacy maintains a comprehensive database of recognized certifying authorities, and shinobi who hold certifications from recognized institutions receive priority in assignments and promotions. Travelers and clanless shinobi face a particularly significant disadvantage in this system, for without access to the training programs of major villages, they must rely on independent certifying authorities, which may not be recognized by the Confederacy. A traveler who has trained under a master in a remote mountain village may possess greater practical skill than any Konoha graduate, but without the proper documentation, they will struggle to convince skeptical village officials of their competence.

### II. Field Honour—The Measure of Performance and Courage

Field Honours are the tangible proof of a shinobi's performance in the field, the hard-won recognition that comes from completing missions, surviving battles, and protecting civilians. They are not given lightly, and they cannot be fabricated through deception or political maneuvering. A field honour is earned through blood, through sweat, and through the kind of work that leaves scars on both body and psyche. The village maintains detailed records of every mission completed, every enemy killed, every civilian saved, and every wound sustained, and these meticulous records form the basis of the entire Field Honour system.

#### Types of Field Honour:

| Category | Examples | Awarding Authority |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Mission Completion | Completion of official missions for the village or Confederacy | Village Mission Assignment Office |
| Combat Citations | Recognition for specific acts of valor or tactical brilliance in combat | Village Combat Review Board |
| Wound Medals | Recognition for wounds sustained in the line of duty | Village Medical Corps |
| Special Operations | Completion of high-risk or high-sensitivity missions | Village Intelligence Division |
| Civilian Protection | Demonstrated commitment to protecting non-combatants | Village Civilian Liaison Office |
| Confederacy Citations | Recognition from the Shinobi Confederacy's central authority | Confederacy Citation Bureau |

The process of earning Field Honours is more straightforward than the certification process, though considerably more dangerous. Mission completions are tracked through official village records, with each mission assigned a rank from E through S, and the shinobi's contribution carefully documented. A genin who completes ten D-rank missions has demonstrated reliability and competence, even if they have not yet faced any truly dangerous situations. A chuunin who completes five B-rank missions has proven their ability to handle significant threats and make difficult decisions under pressure, and such a record alone merits serious consideration for advancement.

Combat citations are awarded by commanding officers or village leadership for specific acts of valor or tactical brilliance, and these citations require detailed documentation of the circumstances and the shinobi's actions. A review board examines each citation carefully before approval, looking for evidence of exceptional courage, sound judgment, and effective leadership, and it will deny citations that do not meet these stringent standards. Wound medals are typically awarded by medical officials or village administration based on the severity of the injury and the circumstances in which it was sustained; a shinobi who is wounded in the line of duty has demonstrated their commitment to the mission and their willingness to risk their life for the village. Special operations, though often classified and shrouded in secrecy, carry significant weight in the promotion process, for a shinobi who has demonstrated the ability to handle sensitive missions is trusted with greater responsibility, and their advancement reflects this trust. Civilian protection honours, often awarded based on testimonial evidence from civilians who have been protected by shinobi, are particularly valued because they demonstrate a shinobi's commitment to the village's core values and its responsibility to those it serves.

#### The Cost of Glory

Field Honours are earned through danger, and the shinobi who has accumulated the most citations is often one who has been in the most life-threatening situations. This is both a testament to their skill and a stark warning about the cost of advancement. A shinobi who has accumulated many Field Honours has likely sustained many injuries, lost many comrades, and carried many psychological burdens. The village recognizes this cost, and promotion boards consider not merely the number of honours but also their nature and the circumstances in which they were earned.

### III. Norm—The Measure of Trust and Reputation

Norms are the intangible yet powerful weight of reputation, the accumulated trust and respect of those who have observed a shinobi's conduct over time. A Norm is not earned through a single heroic act or dramatic mission, but rather through consistent demonstration of reliability, competence, and unquestionable loyalty. It is the endorsement of those who matter, the recognition by one's peers and superiors that a shinobi is worthy of trust and greater responsibility. A Norm is the product of years of consistent performance and the accumulated goodwill of those who have witnessed it firsthand, often taking decades to build but capable of being lost in a single moment of betrayal.

#### Types of Norm:

| Category | Examples | Endorsing Authority |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Jounin Endorsement | A formal recommendation from a jounin or higher-ranking shinobi | The endorsing jounin |
| Kage Endorsement | A formal recommendation from the village Kage | The Kage |
| Settlement Leader Endorsement | Recognition from civilian authorities who have witnessed your work | The settlement leader |
| Peer Recommendation | Endorsement from shinobi of equal or higher rank | The endorsing peers |
| Clan Endorsement | Formal recognition from a major or minor clan | The clan's leadership |
| Confederacy Endorsement | Recognition from the Shinobi Confederacy's central authority | Confederacy Endorsement Bureau |

The process of earning a Norm is both more subjective and more demanding than earning certifications or field honours. A Norm requires someone in a position of authority to formally endorse a shinobi, often through a written recommendation that is added to the shinobi's permanent file. The endorser is expected to have observed the shinobi's performance over an extended period and to be able to provide specific examples of their competence, reliability, and loyalty. Jounin endorsements are typically earned through direct service under a jounin's command; a jounin who has led a shinobi on multiple missions is in a position to evaluate their performance and to recommend them for advancement, and the jounin's endorsement carries significant weight precisely because it is based on direct observation and because the jounin's own reputation is placed on the line.

Kage endorsements are rare and reserved for exceptional service to the village, a powerful statement that the shinobi have demonstrated extraordinary loyalty and competence. Such an endorsement is often the decisive factor in promotions to the highest ranks. Settlement leader endorsements are earned through missions that protect or benefit civilian populations; a settlement leader who has witnessed a shinobi's dedication to protecting their community is often willing to provide a formal endorsement, and this endorsement carries significant weight because it demonstrates the shinobi's commitment to the village's core mission. Peer recommendations are earned through demonstrated reliability and competence; a shinobi who consistently performs well in team missions and who is respected by those who have worked alongside them is likely to receive peer recommendations, and these recommendations demonstrate that the shinobi is trusted by those who matter most.

#### The Weight of Reputation

A Norm is only as valuable as the person who provides it. A Kage endorsement is worth more than a dozen endorsements from ordinary jounin because it represents the highest authority in the village. A clan endorsement from a major clan carries more weight than an entire catalogue of village certifications because it represents the accumulated power and influence of the clan. Travelers and clanless shinobi must navigate this hierarchy carefully, seeking endorsements from those with the most influence while avoiding the appearance of political maneuvering. The Norm system also creates a system of mutual obligation; a shinobi who receives an endorsement from a senior figure is expected to demonstrate loyalty and competence in return, and a shinobi who fails to live up to the expectations of their endorser may find that future endorsements are harder to obtain.

## The Progression of Ranks

### Academy Student (Pre-Genin)

The academy student is not yet a shinobi in the true sense, they are a candidate, their identity defined by potential rather than accomplishment. They have not yet earned the right to serve their village, and their place in the hierarchy is contingent upon their performance in training and their ability to meet the standards set by their instructors. The academy itself functions as both a school and a filter, designed to identify those who have the aptitude to become shinobi while weeding out those who lack either the physical ability or the psychological fortitude for the work. The process is rigorous and often brutal, with students pushed to their physical and mental limits to ensure that only the strongest and most capable graduate.

#### Requirements for Graduation (Genin Promotion)

Promotion to the rank of Genin requires passing the academy's graduation examination, which tests both theoretical knowledge and practical application across the full range of basic shinobi skills, including combat, stealth, chakra control, and tactical thinking. Students who pass receive their dog tags and are officially recognized as shinobi. Students who demonstrate exceptional skill may be promoted early through special programs, requiring a Field Honour and endorsement from a supervising jounin. Students who fail to demonstrate sufficient skill may be held back or dismissed from the academy; while these students have the opportunity to pursue other careers, they are not permitted to serve as shinobi. The timeline for academy training typically spans three to five years, depending on the student's aptitude and the village's needs; those who demonstrate exceptional ability may graduate early, while those who struggle may require additional time.

---

### Genin—The Foundation of Military Strength

Genin are the first true rank of shinobi, the foot soldiers of the shinobi world, the workers who carry out the thousands of missions that keep the villages operational and functional. They are expected to follow orders, work effectively in teams, and survive long enough to learn from their mistakes. Genin are the backbone of the village's military power, and their performance determines the village's ability to complete the complex operations that generate revenue and maintain security. The genin rank is characterized by a high degree of supervision; genin are typically assigned to teams led by experienced jounin, who provide guidance, training, and oversight. The jounin is responsible for the genin's performance and is expected to ensure that they receive the training and experience necessary to advance to the next rank.

#### Requirements for Chuunin Promotion

Promotion to Chuunin requires at least one combat proficiency certification, completion of a minimum of twenty D-rank missions, and ideally at least one Norm. However, Genin may also attempt the Chuunin Exam, a formal competition designed to test combat ability, tactical thinking, and team coordination. The exam is held periodically and is open to genin from all villages, providing an opportunity for candidates to demonstrate their skills in a competitive environment. The exam typically consists of multiple stages: the first stage tests theoretical knowledge and basic skills, the second tests practical skills including combat and stealth, and the final stage tests leadership and decision-making. Successful completion grants a Certification and at least one Field Honour. Alternatively, genin may achieve promotion through accumulating points: D-rank missions provide one point each, C-rank missions provide three points, combat citations provide five points, wound medals provide five points, and special operations provide ten points. Promotion requires 20 points plus at least one Certification. The typical timeframe for genin is two to four years before promotion consideration, though exceptional performance may accelerate this timeline.

---

### Chuunin—The Link Between Command and Action

Chuunin are the middle tier of shinobi society, the squad leaders and mission commanders who keep the village operational. They are expected to demonstrate tactical competence, leadership ability, and the capacity to operate independently when circumstances require it. Chuunin are the vital link between the leadership of the village and the rank-and-file shinobi, responsible for translating strategic objectives into tactical actions and for ensuring that missions are completed successfully. The chuunin rank is characterized by a significant increase in responsibility; chuunin are expected to lead teams of genin, to plan and execute missions, and to make decisions that can affect the lives of their subordinates.

#### Requirements for Jounin Promotion

Promotion to Jounin requires at least one leadership certification and one specialized certification, completion of at least ten C-rank missions or five B-rank missions, and at least one Norm. Alternatively, chuunin may achieve promotion through the Jounin Exam, a formal evaluation that tests tactical command, strategic thinking, and the ability to lead in combat situations. Promotion through point accumulation requires C-rank missions (1 point each), B-rank missions (3 points each), leadership certifications (10 points), specialized certifications (10 points), combat citations (10 points), wound medals (5 points), special operations (15 points), and civilian protection honours (10 points). Promotion requires 40 points plus at least two Certifications and at least one Norm. The typical timeframe is three to six years as a chuunin before promotion consideration, though exceptional performance may accelerate this.

---

### Jounin—The Elite Warriors

Jounin are the elite warriors of the shinobi world, the experts who are trusted with the most dangerous missions and the most difficult decisions. They are expected to demonstrate strategic thinking, leadership ability, and the capacity to operate independently at a high level. A jounin is expected to be able to lead a squad, plan a mission, and adapt to changing circumstances without losing composure or abandoning the mission objectives. The jounin rank is characterized by a high degree of autonomy and responsibility; jounin are expected to operate independently, making decisions that can affect the lives of their subordinates and the security of the village. They are also expected to serve as mentors to younger shinobi, passing on their knowledge and experience to the next generation. In the current era, Jounin have been known to be given autonomy to operate around frontier zones alongside their students, so one could imagine they are closer to a high commander of that region's military.

#### Requirements for Special Jounin or Sannin Promotion

Promotion requires at least two specialized certifications and one leadership certification, completion of at least ten B-rank missions or five A-rank missions, and at least two Norms from different sources. Point-based promotion requires B-rank missions (1 point each), A-rank missions (3 points each), leadership certifications (10 points), specialized certifications (15 points each), combat citations (15 points), wound medals (5 points), special operations (20 points), and civilian protection honours (15 points). Promotion requires 60 points plus at least three Certifications and at least two Norms. The typical timeframe is five to ten years as a chuunin before jounin promotion consideration.

---

### Special Jounin—Masters of Specific Disciplines

Special Jounin are experts in specific fields who have not yet (or may never) achieved full jounin status. They are trusted with missions that require their specific expertise, but they are not expected to command squads or plan complex operations. The Special Jounin rank allows villages to recognize and reward expertise without requiring the full range of skills expected of a regular jounin. Special Jounin are experts in their chosen fields, whether medicine, interrogation, sealing, or any other specialized skill, and they are often called upon to consult on missions that require their expertise.

#### Requirements for Sannin or ANBU Promotion

Promotion requires at least three specialized certifications, completion of at least ten B-rank missions or three A-rank missions in their specialty, and at least one Norm. Point-based promotion requires B-rank specialty missions (2 points each), A-rank specialty missions (5 points each), and specialized certifications (15 points each). Promotion requires 50 points plus at least three specialized Certifications and at least one Norm. The typical timeframe is two to five years before consideration for this rank.

---

### Sannin—The Legendary Warriors

Sannin are legendary warriors whose names are known throughout the Confederacy, the elite of the elite, masters who have transcended ordinary rank structures. They are expected to be able to command armies, plan campaigns, and inspire loyalty through sheer presence and achievement. They are living legends whose names are spoken with reverence and fear. The Sannin rank is not a formal rank in most village structures but rather an informal recognition of exceptional achievement, typically granted to jounin or former jounin who have distinguished themselves through extraordinary feats. Sannin are allowed to leave the village boundaries for prolonged periods of time without incurring the wrath of ANBUs.

#### Requirements for ANBU or Kage Promotion

Promotion requires at least one legendary certification or the direct endorsement of a Kage, completion of at least ten A-rank missions or five S-rank missions, and at least three Norms from different sources. Point-based promotion requires A-rank missions (2 points each), S-rank missions (5 points each), legendary certifications (30 points), Kage endorsements (25 points), major clan endorsements (20 points), S-rank level combat citations (25 points), and S-rank level special operations (30 points). Promotion requires 80 points plus at least one legendary Certification and at least three Norms. The typical timeframe is five to fifteen years as a jounin or special jounin before consideration.

---

### ANBU—The Shadows

ANBU are the shadows of the shinobi world, elite operatives who handle the missions that cannot be acknowledged in public record. They are expected to demonstrate exceptional skill, unwavering loyalty, and the capacity to operate in complete secrecy. ANBU are the village's most trusted agents, given access to classified information and entrusted with the most sensitive missions. The ANBU rank is characterized by a high degree of secrecy and autonomy; ANBU operatives are not subject to the same chain of command as ordinary shinobi, reporting directly to the Kage or to a designated ANBU commander.

---

## The Exception to the Rule

War changes everything. In times of conflict, promotions may be accelerated to fill battlefield vacancies and replace fallen warriors. A genin who distinguishes themselves in battle may be promoted to chuunin immediately, bypassing the usual requirements. A jounin who proves themselves in a major conflict may be elevated to ANBU or even considered for Kage without the usual time in grade. The system is flexible, capable of bending to the pressures of necessity, but this flexibility is also dangerous, those who are promoted too quickly often break under the weight of their new responsibilities.

## Travelers and the Shinobi Confederacy

Travelers, as shinobi without village affiliation, face a fundamentally different set of challenges and constraints. They do not have access to the formal certification system, and they cannot earn Norms from village authorities. Instead, they must rely on reputation and the recognition of independent authorities. The path of the Traveler is one of self-reliance and reputation, where advancement is earned through deeds rather than documents.

### Traveler Equivalent Ranks:

| Formal Rank | Traveler Equivalent | Requirements |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Academy Student | Apprentice | Training under a recognized mentor |
| Genin | Rookie | Completion of basic training and at least one successful mission |
| Chuunin | Outcast | Recognition from a settlement leader or major faction |
| Jounin | Renegade | Recognition from multiple settlement leaders or major factions |
| Special Jounin | Straggler | Recognition as an expert in a specific field |
| Sannin | Legend | Recognition across the Confederacy |
| ANBU | Ghost | Operative status in a major faction's intelligence network |
| Kage | Warlord | Leadership of a significant settlement or faction |

Travelers must build their reputation from scratch, one mission at a time. Recognition from a settlement leader is often the first step toward advancement, providing the traveler with a formal endorsement that can be used to build credibility. Travelers who distinguish themselves may be offered positions in major villages, gaining access to the formal promotion system, this is often the ultimate goal of ambitious travelers, as it provides them with the stability and recognition that they cannot achieve on their own.
